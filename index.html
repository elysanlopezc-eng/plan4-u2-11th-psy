CNB Competency: Constructs rigorous experimental research models to establish cause-and-effect relationships while controlling for confounding variables and methodological artifacts.
Learning Outcome: Differentiates correlation from causation, identifies experimental vs. control groups, isolates confounding variables, and formulates valid conclusions.

Stage I: Warm-up — Correlation vs. Causation

Duration: ~5-10 min

The Ice Cream Paradox

Analyze this statistical fact with your seat partner:

"Data shows that as ice cream sales increase, rates of drowning incidents also rise dramatically."
  1. Does eating ice cream cause drowning?
  2. What hidden 3rd variable (confound) explains both increases?
  3. Why do only controlled experiments allow us to prove true causation?

Hidden Variable (Confound): Summer heat/temperature! Warm weather increases both ice cream consumption and swimming activity.

Core Rule: Correlation indicates a statistical relationship, but ONLY controlled experimentation proves causation by isolating variables.

Anatomy of an Experiment

Independent Variable (IV): The manipulated factor (e.g., dosage, treatment, condition).
Dependent Variable (DV): The measured effect resulting from IV changes.
Experimental vs. Control Group: Group receiving treatment vs. baseline comparison group (or placebo).
Confounding Variable: An uncontrolled outside factor that ruins experiment validity.

Stage II: Experimental Logic & Fictional Case Analysis

Duration: 20 min
Fictional Experiment Case

"The Nootropic Memory Supplement Test"

Dr. Aris testing a new cognitive supplement, "BrainBoost". He places 30 college students in Group A and gives them 100mg of BrainBoost every morning at 8:00 AM before a quiet study session. He places 30 college students in Group B, gives them a sugar pill (placebo) at 8:00 AM, but allows them to listen to music while studying. After 2 weeks, Group A scores 18% higher on a memory recall test than Group B.

1. Identify the IV and DV:

IV: Consumption of "BrainBoost" vs. Placebo (sugar pill).

DV: Memory recall test score (percentage performance).

2. Identify the Major Confounding Variable:

Confound: Background environment during study sessions (Quiet environment vs. Music listening).

Why? The difference in test performance could be caused by music distraction rather than the supplement!

3. How should Dr. Aris standardize this procedure?

Standardization Fix: Keep study conditions 100% identical for both groups (same room, same noise level, same duration, same lighting, random assignment).

Stage III: Team Workshop — Designing a Fair-Test Experiment

Duration: 30 min
Lead Experimentalist Defines IV manipulation and operationalized DV metrics.
Standardization Officer Identifies potential confounds and designs strict control protocols.
Spokesperson Presents experimental design to the class plenary.

Task: Experimental Blueprint Protocol

Select one research prompt below and construct a fully standardized experimental protocol:

Prompt A: "Testing if red light illumination increases typing accuracy under time pressure."
Prompt B: "Testing if taking a 10-minute power nap restores short-term memory after 4 hours of studying."
1. Groups & IV/DV Specify Experimental Group vs. Control Group and measurement units.
2. Random Assignment How will you eliminate participant selection bias?
3. Confound Control List 3 environmental/procedural factors kept 100% constant.
4. Double-Blind Option How will experimenter bias or placebo effect be minimized?

Stage IV: Plenary Presentations & Exit Assessment

Duration: 15 min

Plenary Share (10 min)

Teams pitch their Experimental Blueprint in 90 seconds. Peer reviewers challenge their control mechanisms.

Exit Ticket (5 min)

On your card: "An experiment shows students taking notes by hand score higher on conceptual tests than those typing. What CAN and CANNOT be concluded about intelligence?"
